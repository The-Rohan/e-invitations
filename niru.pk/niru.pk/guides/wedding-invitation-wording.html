Wedding Invitation Wording: Templates in English and Urdu

By Hanzla Nadeem, Niru studio, Lahore · Updated 17 October 2026

Most Pakistani wedding cards, printed or digital, follow the same order: the Bismillah (if the family wants it), who is hosting, the request to attend, the couple's names with their parents' names, the function, the date, time and venue, and finally how to reply. Below are ready-to-use templates for every function, in English and in natural Urdu, to copy and adapt with your own names and details.

The traditional order, explained

Once you know why Pakistani invitations are built the way they are, you can write your own version with confidence.

  1. Bismillah. بسم اللہ الرحمن الرحیم opens many family cards, especially for the Nikah. It is optional and personal; some families use it on the main card only.
  2. The host line. Who is inviting the guest. Traditionally this is the parents or the family, not the couple. Couple-hosted invitations are newer and increasingly common.
  3. The request. The sentence asking the guest to attend: "request the honour of your presence", or in Urdu, "آپ کی شرکت کے منتظر ہیں".
  4. The couple, with parentage. The groom's name, son of his father; the bride's name, daughter of her father. This is the line every Pakistani card keeps even when everything else is simplified, because it tells guests which two families are being joined. The groom's name comes first and the bride's second, even on a card from the bride's family.
  5. The function. Mehndi, Baraat & Nikah, or Walima, named clearly, because many guests attend only some of them.
  6. Date, time and venue. Include the day of the week.
  7. RSVP. A line asking guests to confirm. It was rare on printed cards and is now standard on digital ones, because the family needs the number.

Not every card needs all seven. A Mehndi card can go straight to the function, the date and the venue; a formal Nikah card from an older household usually keeps all seven, in order.

Getting the names and parentage right

Two habits keep formal wording correct. Write the groom's name before the bride's wherever the couple appear together. And check every parent's name and spelling with both families before it is finalised: it is the line most likely to be read twice. If a parent has passed away, most Pakistani cards write "(late)" after the name in English, and مرحوم or مرحومہ in Urdu, rather than leaving them out.

Adapting these for a digital invitation

A printed card has to fit everything onto one folded sheet, so families compress. A digital invitation doesn't have that limit. On a Niru card the Bismillah, the host line and the couple's names sit on the opening screen, and each function (Mehndi, Baraat, Nikah, Walima) gets its own date, time, venue and map, in English and Urdu, switched with one tap. You write the same wording; there is simply room to say it properly.

Questions people ask

How do I write a Nikah card in Urdu?

Open with the Bismillah if you want it, name the host family, then the couple with their parentage, groom first, followed by "نکاح کی تقریب", the date, time and venue, and a line asking guests to confirm. The Baraat & Nikah templates above show the full wording.

Whose name goes first on a Pakistani wedding card, the bride's or the groom's?

The groom's name comes first and the bride's second, in English and in Urdu, on printed and digital cards alike. That holds even when the bride's family is hosting.

What is the correct wording for a Walima invitation?

A Walima invitation comes from the groom's family, names the couple, gives the date, time and venue, and usually says that dinner will be served. See the Walima template above.

How do I politely say no gifts on a Pakistani wedding card?

The gracious version is "Your presence and your duas mean more to us than any gift", which is warmer than a flat "no gifts please" and suits the tone of a Pakistani card.

Do I need the Bismillah on every function's card?

No. Many families use it once, on the main or Nikah invitation, rather than on the Mehndi and Walima too. It is a family choice, not a fixed rule.

Can I just translate my English wording into Urdu?

Use the same structure, but avoid a word-for-word translation. Urdu wedding wording has its own natural phrasing, and a literal translation reads stiffly.
